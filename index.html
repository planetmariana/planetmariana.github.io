Hola, qué tal!

I'm an astrophysicist from Colombia 🇨🇴. My research focuses on the geophysical evolution of magma ocean exoplanets. I want to understand how rocky exoplanets solidify, and how their atmospheres and observability evolve over geological timescales. To do this, I use 1D numerical simulations that couple the magma ocean, the atmosphere and the solid mantle. I work at the Forming Worlds Laboratory under the supervision of Dr. Tim Lichtenberg.

On this website you will find my research, my outreach activities, and my journey through astronomy and nature.

Research projects

During my PhD, I have studied the thermal evolution of lava planets across a wide range of system ages, producing predictions for upcoming observations, and investigated how oxygen fugacity controls mantle melting, with consequences for planetary structure and atmospheric composition. Before my PhD, I completed my master's thesis at the Astronomical Observatory of Geneva, within the Center for Life in the Universe, which led to my first collaborative paper. First-author publications

First author 2026 Submitted to ApJ

Thermal evolution of lava planets across system ages: predictions for Hell of a Survey

M. Sastre, T. Lichtenberg, L. Dang, A. Piette, H. S. Wang, M. López-Morales, et al.

Ultra-short-period rocky planets can be hot enough on their daysides to keep a magma ocean for their whole lives. Using the PROTEUS coupled interior–atmosphere framework, I ran a grid of simulations and sorted the outcomes into six categories, based on how much of the interior stays molten and whether an atmosphere survives that can carry heat around the planet. I then made predictions for five targets of the JWST Cycle 4 Hell of a Survey program (8864): TOI-1807 b, TOI-2260 b, TOI-431 b, TOI-6255 b and TOI-2431 b.

  • Most targets evolve towards a bare-rock end state, with a global melt fraction of 20% or less.
  • Whether an atmosphere survives depends on the escape efficiency. There is a minimum efficiency below which volatile envelopes survive energy-limited escape.
  • A molten surface raises the partial pressure of most species. S2 is the exception: it is enhanced when the surface is solid, so it may trace the interior's melt state.
  • These predictions will help interpret the survey's MIRI-LRS phase curves.
  arXiv:2609.20155
First author 2026 A&A 713, A159

Geophysical and atmospheric implications of fO2-dependent melting on rocky exoplanets

M. Sastre, T. Lichtenberg, L. Soucasse, D. J. Bower, H. Nicholls, I. Kamp

Most models of magma oceans assume Earth-like (oxidised) conditions or very hydrogen-rich (sub-Neptune) ones, which leaves the range in between largely unexplored. In this paper I look at how the redox state of the mantle, measured by its oxygen fugacity (fO2), changes its melting curves. I then follow how those curves shape the planet's thermal evolution, melt fraction and atmosphere. I applied the model to the super-Earth GJ 1132 b using the PROTEUS framework.

  • Reduced mantles tend to build H2–CO-rich atmospheres. Oxidised mantles tend to build thinner H2O–CO2 envelopes.
  • In volatile-poor planets, redox-dependent melting noticeably shifts when the magma ocean crystallises. In volatile-rich planets, the effect on solidification time is small.
  • The volatile inventory and the surface oxygen fugacity are the main controls on the thermal state. Redox-dependent melting is a secondary effect.
  • The predicted atmospheric compositions give spectral signatures that JWST can test.

Outreach activities

I've always wanted to help the astronomical community in my country grow and reach underrepresented communities. For this, I joined the Colombian Network of Astronomy Students (RECA), an association that creates spaces for its members to exchange ideas and experiences, and that runs academic and social-impact projects. I'm on the organising committee of one of them, the Mentorship Program. It helps BSc students get into graduate programs through weekly 1-on-1 meetings that guide them through the application process. The mentors are Colombian astronomers working at institutions all around the world.

About me & 🌋 La Volcánica project 🌋

I had the immense privilege of growing up surrounded by the beautiful nature of the Andes. There, I developed a deep love for this planet's landscapes. During my bachelor's I studied Geoscience alongside Physics, which taught me about the processes that made this planet the way it is. After that, I could never see nature the same way again. I began to notice the beauty of the world around me and how everything is connected. I love hiking, climbing, running, and photographing nature. These experiences have shaped my perspective on life and my work as a scientist.

Somewhere between magma ocean exoplanets and outdoor adventures, I developed a deep passion for volcanoes. I can't visit 55 Cancri e, but I can visit volcanoes here on Earth. That's where my project "La Volcánica" comes from. I plan to visit as many volcanoes as possible, and to explore the full potential of my body and mind in high-altitude environments that make me feel like I'm on another planet.

Below are some of the volcanoes and mountains I've visited since the project started in 2016. I hope to keep adding more volcanoes, mountains and rainforests to this list and share my experiences with you. Random fact about me: I really love documentaries :)
